Develop

E opens the develop panel. Its one-shot verbs sit right under the title as a full-width quick row — Auto, Reset, and a look picker on RAW-paired photos — and a footer row carries Copy, Paste and B / A (the same actions as Shift+C, Shift+V and \). While the panel is closed, resting the pointer on the DEVELOP spine tab (right edge) offers Auto tone and Reset without opening the panel at all. Adjustments render on the GPU, so every slider is realtime at any image size, and everything persists to the XMP sidecar in the standard crs: schema — editors that read it apply your edits on import.

RAW-paired photos develop the RAW

Culling always shows the camera JPEG — but when a photo has a RAW file beside it, opening the develop panel switches the image to a fresh demosaic of the RAW, developed with the file's own white balance and color matrix — and, when culler has a profile for the look the camera shot in, already in that camera look (see The camera look below). A soft render appears near-instantly and sharpens in place a moment later; close the panel and the plain JPEG is back — your adjustments were graded against the RAW rendition, so they are deliberately not re-applied to the already-cooked camera JPEG (they would read overbaked there). The edits aren't lost: they live in the develop panel and in exports, which bake a graded photo from the RAW (a photo you haven't graded exports its camera JPEG instead — the picture you culled — even while this panel shows its RAW) — and a quiet [edited] tag in the top-left overlay row marks any photo carrying RAW edits you can't currently see. Only the crop follows you out of the panel: geometry means the same thing on both files. The tag next to the panel's title says which one you're looking at: [RAW] or [JPEG] — it always describes the pixels actually on screen, so a photo whose RAW is still decoding (or downloading) reads [JPEG] until the real thing lands. The same moment, an accent-colored [RAW] tag appears over the photo itself, in the top-left row next to [stack] and [before] — inside a stack of mixed RAW and JPEG members it appears and disappears per member as you step through, so there's never a doubt about which file you're grading.

The camera look

A RAW opens in the camera's own look, matched to its JPEG: the picture style recorded in the file (Vivid today) and an exposure fitted to that shot, so switching from the camera JPEG to the RAW changes the picture as little as possible. The camera JPEG stays up until that look is ready, then the RAW takes over. While the panel is open, the photos either side of the current one are prepared in the background, so stepping to them lands on their look straight away.

This is a starting point, not an edit: every slider sits at 0, the photo isn't marked as edited, and nothing is written until you change something. Your sliders work on top of the look, and Reset returns to it. To start from the flat RAW instead, choose Neutral in the look picker — or, for every RAW you haven't edited, set RAW opens in to Neutral in Settings (Cmd+, → RAW files). Photos you developed before this release keep the picture they had; Reset puts them on the camera look too.

J on an untouched photo exports what you see: a RAW with its camera JPEG beside it ships that JPEG, and a RAW on its own renders in the camera look, fitted against the preview the camera stored inside it.

The look approximates the camera's rendering, so some difference remains: in-camera dynamic range optimisation, local adjustments, noise reduction and sharpening aren't reproduced. A RAW from a camera culler has no look for opens on its JPEG's exposure alone — the picker reads Camera exposure — with contrast and colour left flat, the neutral rendition your adjustments build on. Lens correction is applied from the file's own parameters (see the slider list below). The histogram and its tone curve describe what's on screen, look included.

Photos with no RAW (plain JPEG, HEIC) keep developing the JPEG exactly as before, and a RAW that can't be decoded falls back the same way — the tag tells the truth either way. On a server source the RAW original downloads first ("Downloading RAW" in the pending-work pill); the panel opens instantly on the server preview and the tag flips to [RAW] when the real pixels land. The download is a one-time cost per photo.

White balance becomes real in RAW mode. On a JPEG, Temp and Tint are relative nudges (−100..+100 around 0) — a JPEG has no true white balance left to change. On a RAW, the Temp slider turns into an actual Kelvin scale (2000–25000, log-spaced so the everyday 3000–9000 band gets most of the travel) whose home position is the file's As Shot white balance; hover the slider to see it, double-click to return to it. Tint stays −100..+100, zero meaning As Shot. The number format is the tell: "5500K" is a RAW, "+12" is a JPEG. The two settings are stored separately per photo — RAW-mode edits write the real crs:Temperature/crs:Tint fields, JPEG-mode edits keep the Incremental pair, and neither ever touches the other.

The histogram is a control

The live RGB histogram at the top reflects your adjustments, and you can grab it directly:

  • It has five zones, left to right: Blacks, Shadows, Exposure (the wide middle), Highlights and Whites, split by four faint lines. Drag in a zone to move its slider; the tone you press on tracks the pointer — it feels like dragging the histogram mass itself. The histogram's bottom line names the zone and its value as you go, and pressing a zone aims its slider. The small triangles in the two top corners are the clipping indicators' own (below) — a press there never moves Blacks or Whites.
  • See clipping as it happens. While you drag a zone — or move Exposure, Highlights, Whites, Shadows, Blacks, Curve highlights or Curve shadows, by dragging or by scrolling over the slider — the bottom right of the histogram reads clipped 0.8 %: how much of the photo that end blows out (the highlight sliders, the middle and right zones) or crushes (the shadow sliders, the two left zones). It counts the same pixels the corner triangles light on, turns amber past 0.5 %, and goes a moment after you stop.
  • Double-click a zone to reset that parameter; Shift+double-click the Blacks or Whites zone to set it to the clipping point (see Whites / Blacks).
  • The histogram is computed over the cropped region, so it describes the photo you're actually keeping.
  • It shows what's on screen, untouched photos included: an untouched photo's white sits at the right edge, where it renders. On a RAW, the highlights brighter than the display's white — detail the sensor kept — appear where the picture puts them, packed toward the right edge, rather than stacked in one spike.

The curve

The white line across the histogram is the tone curve: for every input tone (left = black, right = white) it shows the tone your sliders turn it into (bottom = black, top = white). Untouched, it lies on the faint diagonal: untouched, white stays white. Push Exposure up and the line bends over near the top instead of running into it, so the brightest tones compress toward white rather than clipping. On a RAW the line follows the RAW's own rendering, so with the camera's look on it keeps climbing toward white instead of going flat across the top. Raise Exposure and the whole line lifts; add Contrast and it steepens into an S; pull Highlights and the top end bends down. While you drag a tonal slider the line brightens to the accent color, and dragging one of the four Tone curve sliders also thickens the stretch of the curve that slider owns, so you can see exactly where "Curve darks" ends and "Curve lights" begins. White balance, Vibrance/Saturation and Vignette don't move the line — they change color or corners, not tones.

Working a slider

Each slider is a label, its value and a track. The short tick on the track marks the slider's default — where a double-click returns it — so a slider you haven't touched is just the line and its tick. Move it and a blue fill grows from the tick to the value, a small knob marks where it sits, and the value brightens; sliders that go both ways say which way they went (+15, −8, and Exposure in stops, +0.67 EV). Hovering a row shows the knob full size and draws a field around the value: click the value and type an exact number (Enter keeps it, Esc puts the old one back).

Sliders drag relative to where you grab — the value moves with your pointer's travel, damped so a wide drag is a moderate change, which makes fine adjustment easier and means a stray click never jumps the value (Settings → Slider drag speed sets how far a drag goes).

Hold ⌥ (Option) for fine control and ⇧ (Shift) for coarse: while it's held, a drag moves the value a tenth as far — or four times as far — as it otherwise would, and so does dragging in the histogram (the scroll wheel and the step keys below take a finer or a coarser step). The knob shrinks for fine and grows for coarse, and a fine or coarse caption appears beside the value. Press or let go of the key in the middle of a drag and the value carries on from where it is — it never jumps.

The aimed slider

One slider at a time can be aimed — its row tints, a blue bar marks it at the card's edge, and its knob stays visible. Everything precise acts on the aimed slider: the keys, the scroll wheel, and typing a value. A slider is aimed when you let go of a drag on it, click its track or its value, or press in its zone of the histogram (Shadows, Exposure or Highlights) — and from the keyboard:

The scroll wheel edits only the aimed slider (with the pointer over it), or any slider while you hold ⌥; anywhere else in the panel it scrolls the panel, so a swipe meant to reach Color Mix can't move a slider on the way. A notch is half a key step — 0.05 EV, or 2 on a ±100 slider (⌥ and ⇧ scale it too) — and a trackpad moves the same amount per distance, smoothly. Whatever a swipe starts on keeps it until the wheel falls quiet for a moment, so a flick's momentum never jumps from the panel onto a slider or off one.

The aim stays while you step through your photos — nudge Exposure on each frame of a series without reaching for the mouse. It goes when you press Esc, close the panel, collapse its section, enter crop, Compare, Survey or the Grid, or pick up the white-balance eyedropper; inside a series, the Look tab keeps it and the Align tab lets go.

The line at the foot of the panel, above Copy, Paste and B / A, changes with what you're doing: at rest it names the aim keys and W, while you drag a slider it lists the modifiers, and with a slider aimed it lists that slider's keys.

Sections

The sliders live in four collapsible sections — Tone (Exposure, Contrast, Highlights, Shadows), Color (Temp, Tint, Vibrance, Saturation), Color Mix (per-hue Hue/Saturation/Luminance) and Curve & Effects (the tone curve regions, Vignette, and — while a RAW is on screen — Denoise and Lens correction). Click a header to collapse or expand it; the app remembers your arrangement across restarts. Option-click (⌥) a header to see only that section: it opens and the other three fold away (the History and Snapshots cards stay as they are). On a fresh install only Tone is open — the every-photo section — keeping the pane compact. A collapsed section can never hide an edit silently: a small accent dot on the header means something inside is off its default.

Every slider in the pane has its own name — the tone curve's four say Curve highlights / Curve lights / Curve darks / Curve shadows, so they never read as Tone's Highlights and Shadows.

The sliders

  • Temp / Tint — white balance: Temp seesaws red/blue (negative cools, positive warms), Tint moves the green–magenta axis. Neutral grays keep their brightness while the color shifts.

  • Pick gray point (Q) — the white-balance eyedropper, at the top of the Color card. Click it (or press Q), then click something in the photo that should be neutral gray (a gray card in the shot is ideal): culler sets Temp and Tint so that point renders neutral — in Kelvin on a RAW, as slider offsets on a JPEG — and the status line names the result. A blown or crushed spot is refused ("that point is clipped") and the picker stays armed so you can try another; Esc cancels, and moving to another photo quietly puts the tool away. To carry one reference across a whole series shot in the same light, pick once and use Shift+C / Shift+V to copy the settings along.

  • Exposure — brightness in EV, in linear light. Pushed up, the brightest tones roll off smoothly into white instead of clipping flat, and colours keep their hue as they brighten (skin doesn't turn yellow, skies don't turn cyan). On a RAW, pulling Highlights down also brings back detail the sensor kept above the display's white.

  • Contrast — an S-curve around the midtones.

  • Highlights / Shadows — recover the bright end or lift the dark end. Each follows the pixel's own tone at any strength, so the histogram shows exactly what you get. Highlights works on the bright tones below white and leaves white itself alone: pulled all the way down it never turns a blown sky or window grey, never swaps light and dark tones, and draws no halo round clouds. On a RAW, pulling it down also brings back detail the sensor kept above white.

  • Whites / Blacks — set the two ends. Whites up stretches the brightest tones toward white (all the way up, it clips the top on purpose); Whites down pulls them back — on a RAW it brings in detail the sensor kept above white, on a JPEG it dims white. Blacks down deepens the darkest tones toward black; Blacks up lifts black itself into a soft grey. Neither ever swaps light and dark tones. Shift+double-click either one (or its histogram zone) to set it to the clipping point: Whites so the brightest tenth of a percent just reaches the clipping level, Blacks so the darkest tenth just reaches black — the history names it "Whites (auto)". A plain double-click still resets it; hovering either row says so.

  • Vibrance / Saturation — Vibrance boosts muted colors more than already-vivid ones (protecting what's already saturated); Saturation is uniform. −100 Saturation is black & white. On a RAW in the camera's look, Saturation scales the look's own colour, so −100 is still black & white and every step up to +100 still adds colour.

  • Color Mix — eight per-hue bands (Red · Orange · Yellow · Green · Aqua · Blue · Purple · Magenta), each centred on the colour it's named for as you see it — Yellow moves yellows, skin sits in Orange, and a colour between two bands is shared by both. Hue rotates a range of hues, Saturation deepens or mutes it, and Luminance brightens or darkens it — leaving the other colors alone. Reach for it to lift a sky without touching skin, or calm one loud color. It works on any photo, RAW or JPEG, and is part of what gives a camera look its per-hue colour rendering rather than just its tone. A toggle at the top of the section picks how you steer the eight bands — and it remembers your choice:

    • Sliders — pick a swatch, then adjust its three rows, named for the colour — Orange hue, Orange saturation, Orange luminance. Precise and familiar, one band at a time.
    • Graph — one curve over a rainbow hue axis: drag a point up or down to boost or cut that hue. A Hue / Sat / Lum tab picks what the curve controls; you see all eight bands at once.
    • All — the three curves (Hue, Saturation, Luminance) stacked over a single shared hue bar, nothing hidden.

    All three write the same values — the toggle only chooses the interface.

  • Tone curve (Curve highlights / Curve lights / Curve darks / Curve shadows) — four regions of a parametric curve, for shaping after the basic tone is set: lift the darks a touch, ease the lights, without touching exposure.

  • Vignette — post-crop corner darkening (0 to −100). It follows the crop, so the falloff is always centered on your final frame.

  • Denoise (RAW mode only) — grain reduction, baked into the RAW render before tone. It starts off (0): smoothing costs fine detail, so you turn it up only on the photos that need it. Hover the row to see a suggested strength for the photo's ISO — a base-ISO frame needs none, a dim high-ISO frame a real dose; double-click returns to 0. Because the smoothing is part of the demosaic, the photo refines a beat after you release the slider, behind the same "Refining detail" notice as opening the pane. Judge the result at 100% (Z).

  • Lens correction (RAW mode only) — a checkbox at the bottom of Curve & Effects, on by default whenever the RAW carries the camera's own correction data (Sony bodies bake per-shot distortion, chromatic aberration and vignetting parameters into every ARW — the same data the camera used for its JPEG, so no lens profiles to manage). With it on, the RAW's framing matches the corrected camera JPEG; turn it off to see the naked lens. Files without correction data show a quiet "no profile" note instead.

Clipping warnings: W

W paints clipping warnings straight onto the photo: pixels blown to white glow red, pixels crushed to black glow blue — the classic clipping overlay, applied to the final render (so a heavy vignette can legitimately warn in the corners). The histogram's top corners carry small always-on indicators (shadows left, highlights right) that light up when a real part of the photo reaches the level the overlay paints — at least one pixel in 2,000 — so a few specular glints or a dead pixel don't count, while anything big enough to see glowing lights its corner. Clicking one toggles the overlay, and while it's active they get an accent ring and the top overlay shows [clipping]. The warnings are near-clip rather than exact-255: red marks any channel at display level 250 or above, blue a pixel whose channels are all at 5 or below. Exports never include them.

Hold ⌥ (Option) to see exactly what clips. While a highlight slider — Exposure, Highlights, Whites or Curve highlights — is aimed, being dragged, stepped or scrolled, holding ⌥ replaces the photo with a clipping mask: the picture dims and only the clipping pixels show, each in the colour of the channels it lost — red, green or blue alone, yellow, cyan or magenta for two, and white for nothing left in any channel. On a RAW, a pixel blown on screen that the sensor still has detail for shows gold instead of white, so pulling Highlights or Exposure down visibly eats the gold first. On a shadow slider — Shadows, Blacks or Curve shadows — the mask is a white page with the crushed pixels in darker inks and black for nothing left. The panel's foot names the legend, the histogram's readout counts that end, and letting go of ⌥ brings the photo back. The mask wins over W while both are on, and exports never include it. Hovering alone never shows it (⌥ is also how a dragged photo switches between Move and Copy).

Tap to latch, hold to peek. A quick tap of W turns the warnings on (or off) and leaves them. Hold W instead to look for as long as you hold it — let go and they're gone again; with the warnings already on, holding W hides them for the look. \ (before / after) works the same way. While you move a highlight or shadow slider, the histogram also counts what clips as you go (clipped 0.8 %, see the histogram).

On a RAW render, blown isn't always gone. The sensor usually keeps detail past the point where the display clips, so in [RAW] mode the warnings split: gold marks highlights that are blown on screen but still recoverable — pull Highlights or Exposure down and real detail comes back — while red stays reserved for pixels the sensor itself clipped in the file. Even those pull down gracefully: where only some color channels clipped (a bright sky almost always loses green first), the missing channel is reconstructed from the surviving ones, so blown whites come back as white — not the pink cast naive RAW converters show — and only fully clipped pixels settle for neutral gray. Crushed blacks stay blue in both modes. The histogram's highlight indicator and the [clipping]/[recoverable] overlay tag follow the same split, with red winning whenever any true sensor clip is present. The split exists only while the RAW render is on screen — with the panel closed, W describes the plain camera JPEG, so a sky can read blown while culling yet recoverable the moment you press E. That's the two files talking, not a bug.

Auto: Shift+A

Shift+A (or the panel's Auto button) applies auto tone: a starting point computed from the histogram of what you see (exposure toward a balanced midtone, highlight/shadow rescues where mass piles up at the ends, a touch of contrast for flat frames), a nudge that only touches the four tonal sliders and leaves white balance, presence, curve and vignette alone. On a RAW in its camera look it's measured on that look and sits on top of it — the look stays, the sliders carry the nudge. It's an explicit press, so it applies over whatever's there; Reset returns to the camera look (or clears everything on a photo without one). To go back to the camera look after choosing another, pick Camera look (default) in the look picker.

Camera looks

The look picker (RAW mode only, the dropdown next to Auto and Reset) shows the look under the sliders and switches it in one click — a fixed, consistent grade rather than a per-photo guess. The camera's own look is where a RAW starts — the closed picker reads, say, Vivid · camera — and Camera look (default), the first row, puts the photo back on it. Neutral starts flat. Pick a different look and it's applied immediately: the develop sliders are set to that profile (contrast, a saturation push that ramps in with a color's own vividness — real color gets the boost, mist and gray cloud stay quiet — a gentle highlight rolloff and deepened shadows, and the per-hue Color Mix that gives the look its character), the RAW's As-Shot white balance is kept (the same white balance the camera used for its JPEG), and only exposure is matched to the scene. Because the look itself is fixed, every photo gets the same character — no railing, no surprises — and you can refine from there with the ordinary sliders; Reset undoes it. The picker appears the moment real RAW pixels are on screen (the same moment the [RAW] tag lights up).

It knows what the camera shot. If the RAW records an in-body creative look (Sony bodies do), culler reads it and opens the RAW in the matching profile — so a Sony shot in Vivid opens in Vivid · camera. A photo you've already graded keeps its grade. If the camera used a look there's no profile for yet, the picker names it with a "no profile yet" line rather than guessing, and the RAW opens on its JPEG's exposure alone.

A look is a starting point, not a pixel-for-pixel copy of the camera JPEG: in-camera noise reduction and sharpening aren't reproduced, and a few scenes (blown light sources, very high ISO) diverge more. Vivid ships today; more looks are on the way, and you can add your own — drop a <name>.conf in ~/.culler/looks/ (a name= line sets its display name) and it joins the list.

Matching a series: M

Match series makes a set of photos belong together: the same frame — every subject in the same place, so the set scrolls like a stop-motion film — and, when you want it, the same look. Stand on the photo that should lead and press M: up to 24 photos around it freeze into a set, and the one you were on becomes the reference. Nothing changes yet. Entering never touches your photos, and nothing is written until you press Done.

For the length of the session the right-hand panel is the series panel (screenshot). Its head names the reference and counts the photos; under it sit two tabs — Align for the frames, Look for the tone — and E flips between them (so does a click). At the foot, always in the same place under both tabs: one line saying exactly what Done will apply, Discard (only once there is something to lose) and Done. Every session opens on Align — or on Look, if that's where you'd rather start: Settings → Match series → Match series opens on decides it for every session. E switches within a session; the next one opens on the setting's tab again.

A series is rarely a neat run. A set shot weeks apart — a pregnancy progression, a growing kid on the same bench — lives scattered across your library, and the frozen set follows whatever you're looking at (it's just as happy with a moment). So build the view first: select the series (Y on each photo, or Cmd/Shift+click), press Shift+Y to show just the selection, then M — the set is now exactly your series, in date order, and the photo you were standing on is the reference. A selection larger than 24 photos is cut at the cap; the entry line says so (24 of 31 selected photos) — narrow the selection to match exactly the set you mean.

Walk the set with ←/→ (the filmstrip marks the reference with a solid accent ring). P/X/ratings/labels still work on the focused photo — a bad frame gets rejected without leaving.

Aligning the frames

The Align tab is where a session starts (unless Settings says Look). Its Landmarks card says where the series stands and offers the next step as one button — the same thing Shift+C does from anywhere in the session:

  • Place landmarks — nothing has points yet. It opens the reference.
  • Continue at … (it names the photo) — "6 points · 3 of 5 photos have them": the reference's points are there and some photos still need theirs. It opens the first one that does.
  • Edit landmarks — every photo has its points. It opens the reference's points again, to move, add or remove them (see Editing the reference's points below); C still re-aligns the focused photo by hand.

Landmark align (Shift+C). Align by matching points. On the reference, click 2–8 distinctive points (an ankle, the nose, a hip ridge). Enter then walks the series: each photo opens in a three-pane layout — the reference with its numbered dots already in place, your photo, and the live result. Click the same numbered points on your photo; from the second point the third pane shows the solved placement — position, size and tilt all at once — as a faint overlay on the reference's frame. A point you can't see on this photo (an ankle out of frame) is no problem: Tab skips it, and any two shared points are enough — the numbered chips above your photo show what's placed and aim the next click. A magnifier follows the cursor for precise placement; drag a dot to adjust, ⌫ undoes the last one, and a right-click on a dot (or its hover ×) removes it (on the reference that removes the number from every photo). Enter accepts and moves to the next photo that needs points (after the last it returns to the series panel); Back to series (C) keeps the placement too and stops there; Esc backs out without it. Shift+C instead drops to the drag view with the solve applied, for hand fine-tuning; Clear landmarks in the crop pane forgets the reference's whole set, or just this photo's points. culler still only crops, never warps — if the pose truly changed, the dots can't all land exactly, and the preview shows that honest compromise rather than pretending.

Editing the reference's points. Once every photo is aligned, Edit landmarks (or Shift+C) opens the reference's landmark view with its points in place: drag one to move it, click to add one, right-click (or its hover ×) to remove one. Each photo's points are paired to the reference's numbers, so the rest of the series keeps its work: a moved or an added point takes no photo's points away, and a removed point only takes away that one pair. Enter then visits just the photos an edit left with fewer than two pairs — usually none — and the Landmarks card says the same ("1 of 5 photos have them").

The photos that keep two pairs follow the edit: as soon as you're back at the series, every photo whose crop is still exactly the one its landmarks gave it is re-aligned to the new points — "3 photos re-aligned to the edited landmarks" — so K previews it, Done applies it ("2 landmarks and 3 photos re-aligned") and Discard takes it back with everything else. A photo whose crop you've adjusted by hand since its landmarks placed it keeps your crop ("1 kept its hand-tuned crop"). So does a photo aligned with a culler older than this behavior: nothing recorded the crop its landmarks gave it, so nothing can prove it untouched, and it is treated as hand-tuned ("1 aligned by an earlier version kept its crop"). To re-solve such a photo against the new points — once; from then on it follows — focus it, press C then Shift+C for its landmark view, and Enter.

Landmarks are saved with the photos. Done applies the crops and the points (the reference's set, and each accepted photo's points, paired to the reference's numbers); Discard drops both. Next time you open a series on the same reference — say, two new photos joined it — the Align tab reads "3 of 5 photos have them", the dots are back the moment a pane opens, and Shift+C lands straight on the first photo that still needs its points. A photo's points belong to the reference they were placed against; re-anchoring with R loads whatever the new reference has stored — which is why R is refused while the session still holds landmark points you haven't applied ("can't re-anchor — it would drop the landmark work on 2 photos · M commits it first"). On an Immich source the points are kept in culler's state on this computer and reach the server with the photo's next J. Points on their own never make a photo unsaved: a reference you placed points on but didn't crop or grade stays out of the "N unsaved" count, Shift+U, Save edits and the reminder on quit — saving it would only upload a new copy of an untouched photo. Its points travel the next time you save it for a crop or a grade.

Points stay on the photo, not on the picture in the pane. A server photo you've saved with J shows culler's own render — already cropped — until its RAW has downloaded, and the RAW after that. Landmark points belong to the photo itself: a point you click on the render sits on the same feature once the RAW appears, still does after the next J crops the photo differently, and a point the render crops away is simply not drawn (it still counts for the alignment). The RAW never swaps in while you're holding the mouse button down; it appears as soon as you let go. Until culler has read back what that photo's last J saved, its pane doesn't take clicks — the status line says "still reading this photo's saved edit — try again in a moment". Points an earlier version of culler placed on a render are moved onto the photo by themselves when culler can prove where they belong. When it can't, the walk stops at that photo with "its saved points are from an earlier version — check they sit on the same features · its crop is kept": drag any dot that's off and press Enter, or move on — the photo keeps the crop it had until you press Enter there. A series saved by this version and opened in an older one may show the points on such photos in the wrong place.

By hand (C). The Frame card's By hand — C — works without any points. On the reference it's the ordinary crop tool: set the frame that leads the series. On any other photo it opens the align view: the reference's finished frame fills the screen, solid, and never moves — your photo rides on top as a faint overlay, pre-placed from the reference's frame (never over a crop the photo already carries). Now manipulate your photo, not a window: drag it until the subject sits where the reference's does, scroll, pinch or ↑/↓ to size it in the frame, Cmd/Ctrl+arrows to nudge it precisely (Shift makes every step coarser), ←/→ to straighten, R for quarter turns. A quiet outline shows where your photo's actual edges are — its corners carry drag handles that size the photo too, so the reference's frame itself stays free of controls. The overlay is exactly what will be applied — \ flips to a solid preview of the result and back. A caption names which is which (solid: <reference> · faint: <this photo>). The aspect stays locked to Matched in the crop pane (pick another ratio to deliberately break ranks; a [custom] tag says you have), and when a tighter-framed photo needs its subject placed beyond its own edge, just keep dragging — the uncovered part exports as white padding. Reset in the pass means back to the reference's placement, not back to full. The subject moved between sessions, so placement stays yours per photo; the shape is what stays identical.

Inside a pass the crop pane's button reads Back to series, not Done, and says why right above it: nothing is applied until Done in the series panel. It closes the pass and keeps what you did, in memory with the rest of the session.

Matching the look

Press E for the Look tab (or set Settings to open there): the develop panel, shaped for a set (screenshot). It edits the reference, and every slider move carries the whole linked set with it — each photo keeping its own hidden offset, so the group shifts together instead of snapping to identical numbers.

Press Match when you want the fit: every linked photo's tone is fitted toward the reference — exposure, contrast and the two tail rescues, each from its own starting point. The fit reads each frame's histogram and its EXIF exposure settings, so a frame shot a stop darker (faster shutter, smaller aperture) gets the physical correction — exposure, and only exposure, when that is all that differs — while a frame where the scene genuinely changed (a shadow crossing, more sky) is only nudged, never forced — a sudden shadow is real, not a mistake. A tail rescue never moves any tone further than the difference it is nudging, and a part of the frame that is pure black or blown white on either side isn't chased at all: how far past the clip it went can't be measured. Don't like the result? Reset all puts every photo's tone back to how it was when you entered, without leaving and without touching the alignment. It's the same "starting point, not a pixel match" honesty as a camera look: the fit gets the set close, your eyes make the call. Check any photo against its original with \.

  • R makes the focused photo the new reference (a fit you've run re-fits against it; before the first Match, re-anchoring changes nothing).
  • L unlinks the focused photo — its sliders are its own from there — or links it back. Unlinked members wear a thin amber ring in the filmstrip and an [unlinked] tag (the reference's ring is solid blue; linked members have none). A RAW-paired photo that already carries a develop grade starts unlinked, so a fit can never overwrite deliberate work; L opts it in.
  • Q neutralizes the focused photo's white balance: click something gray. Photos from different sessions rarely share a color cast, and the tone fit deliberately leaves white balance alone (it can't tell a cast from a real scene change) — so for a series, sample each photo first, walk to the next, repeat, then press Match: the eyedropper does the color, the fit does the tone.

These are the Look tab's own: the ring, the tag and the live thumbnails only show there (or once you've changed the tone), and on the Align tab L and Q just point you to E. Someone who came to align sees none of it.

RAW-paired members' filmstrip thumbnails warm up when the Look tab first opens — a few seconds, one at a time (the pending pill counts them: "Preparing thumbnails") — into live renders that track every slider move, so a matched set visibly matches without leaving the panel. They revert to their normal plain thumbnails the moment the session ends. Each RAW shows in its own camera look, as it does outside the session, and Done saves every changed photo with that look kept under its sliders.

The panel changes shape for the session: Auto and the look picker step aside (they'd drag the whole group through an unrelated fit), Copy and Paste leave its foot (a paste would write the photo at once, and a series writes nothing until Done — Shift+V in a session says so), and the buttons become Match — the fit itself, run when you ask, re-run after a re-anchor — and Reset all. Until you've pressed Match, slider moves still carry the linked set — each photo shifting by the same amount from its own values, no fit implied. If a photo's fitted tone hits a slider's end of travel, it stops tracking the group on that axis: a small amber dot appears in its thumbnail corner and a [clamped] tag joins the panel header while it's focused — drag back toward center and it reconnects.

Previewing and finishing

Seeing the series whole. K — or Preview on the Align tab — lays the frozen set out as a grid: every photo with its live crop and tone, exactly what the finished series will look like side by side. It's read-only: click a photo to jump focus to it (the grid closes), K or Esc just closes. It's the registration check before anything is applied, and the place to spot the one frame a fit got wrong.

Nothing is written until Done. Everything in a session — aligned frames, landmark points, matched tone, gray-point samples — lives in memory. The line at the panel's foot always says what Done (M) would apply: "nothing changed yet", "2 photos aligned — applied when you press Done", "3 photos matched + aligned — …". Done writes each changed photo once, with one history entry apiece — Aligned to and the reference's name for a photo whose frame or points changed, Match look when its tone did, Landmarks · 3 points for a reference whose points changed — and the status line confirms it ("series done — 2 photos aligned"). With nothing changed it simply leaves.

Undoing a series. Each entry holds the photo's whole state — its frame and its landmark points — so one Cmd/Ctrl+Z on an aligned photo takes both back, and the next walk stops on it again. A second Done on the same photo is a second step: the first alignment stays a point you can return to. Undo is per photo, as everywhere: undoing the reference's points never moves the photos aligned to them, and the status line says so ("… — 4 aligned photos keep their crops").

Discard (Esc) puts every photo back exactly as it was. On an untouched session it leaves at once; once anything has changed, the first Esc — or a click on Discard 2 — only arms it: the button turns red and reads Discard 2?, and the status line names what would go and the other way out ("discard 2 aligned photos? Esc again confirms — or M to apply them"). A second Esc or click discards; any other key or a click anywhere else stands it down. A second press, that is: holding a key down never counts twice in here, so one long Esc backs out of exactly one thing (the landmark view, the crop pass, a value you were typing, or — from the panel — it arms), and a held M or Enter acts once. Quitting culler mid-session applies, like Done.

Applied crops show at once in every view — on photos that already carry an uploaded edit too — so scrolling the series plays like a stop-motion film. On an Immich source Done is not J: the photos then read unsaved until you press J on each (or save them together from the Batch panel), which is what re-renders the server copies with the new framing.

Copying settings across photos: Shift+C / Shift+V

Shift+C copies the current photo's develop settings and crop (including rotation); Shift+V pastes them onto the current photo. A moment shot in identical light becomes one keystroke per photo. Inside a series session neither applies: Shift+C is the landmark walk there, and a paste — which writes the photo at once — is refused until you press Done or Discard.

Edit history & snapshots

Every edit is remembered. Cmd/Ctrl+Z undoes the last change, Shift+Cmd/Ctrl+Z redoes it (the rail's ⋯ menu has Undo and Redo rows too, named after the step) — and it's not just develop sliders: ratings, flags, color labels, crop, straighten, a quarter turn (R), the landmark points of a series, a pasted look, a Reset, they all step back and forth the same way. Each step restores the whole photo to exactly how it stood — its frame and its points together — and writes that state back to the sidecar, so undo is as durable as any other edit. (Steps recorded by a version of culler from before landmark points joined the history don't know the points: stepping onto one leaves them as they are.)

One undo step is one stretch of work on one control. Drag Exposure, look, nudge it again — that's one step, and a held key or a burst of the scroll wheel counts the same. The stretch ends when you pause for 8 seconds, move to another control, go to another photo, or quit; the next change starts a new step. So five Exposure tweaks spread over a coffee undo one at a time, not all at once. A named action — Reset (develop and crop each on their own), Auto, a look, a paste, restoring a snapshot — is always a step of its own, however quickly the next one follows.

The develop panel shows the full trail. Two cards at the bottom of the panel — collapsed by default, click the header to open — make it visible:

  • History — the running list of what you did, newest at the top, each row dotted in its action's color (a rating in gold, a pick in green, a develop move in blue). ‹ Undo and Redo › buttons sit above it, and the row marked current is where you are now. Click any row to restore that state — rewind six edits in one click. Restoring is non-destructive: it lands as a new entry on top, so nothing in between is lost, and the restore itself is undoable. The very first entry is always Opened — the photo as it was when you started. Hovering a row reveals a + on its right: click it to save that exact state as a snapshot (named after the entry) without leaving where you are — handy for keeping a version you might want back.
  • Snapshots — history is a timeline; a snapshot is a bookmark you name. Dial in a look you like, type a name (or leave it blank for an auto-numbered one) and press Save — that exact state is pinned. Later, however far you've wandered, Restore brings it back in one click (and the restore is itself an undoable step). Click a snapshot's name to rename it, or × to remove it. Unlike history, snapshots are never pruned — they're the states you deliberately kept. A snapshot keeps the photo's landmark points too.

The whole trail lives in the photo's sidecar (culler:History / culler:Snapshots), so it survives closing and reopening culler — pick a photo back up next week and its history and snapshots are right where you left them. History is capped generously (about two thousand steps, always keeping the oldest Opened anchor); in practice it's effectively complete. Undo isn't available while a crop or a series session is open — finish or cancel it first (those have their own Esc).

Overwriting the JPEG ends the trail. Shift+J bakes your edits into the camera JPEG, so every earlier step describes pixels that now live in the .orig backup. The overwrite adds an Overwrote JPEG step, and undo stops there with "the JPEG was overwritten here — the earlier states belong to P0001.JPG.orig"; a click on an older row, or a snapshot saved before the overwrite, is refused the same way. Everything you do afterwards undoes and redoes as usual, back down to that step.

Editing photos from an Immich source

Photos from a folder save every edit the instant you make it (to the sidecar next to the file). Photos from an Immich source work a little differently, because there's no sidecar on the server — so culler keeps your develop grade, crop, and edit history in Immich itself, and writes them when you press J (the same key that renders the edit and uploads it). Until you press J, the grade lives only in this session, and a quiet [unsaved] tag shows (in the develop panel's title row, and on the photo when the panel is closed) to remind you. Ratings and flags are the exception — those save to Immich immediately, as always.

The practical upshot:

  • Press J to keep a grade. Then it's stored in Immich; reopen culler later — or open the same library on another computer — and your grade, crop, and full history come right back.
  • A grade you never J'd lives only on this computer. Develop a photo, then close culler without pressing J: the grade is still there next time (culler keeps it in its own state file), but it never reached Immich — open the same library elsewhere and it isn't there, and a later J from another machine would replace the render without it. The [unsaved] tag, the corner's "N unsaved" chip (with Shift+U to see every such photo), the sources pane's per-server count and the reminder on quit all point at the same thing: press J when the grade is done.
  • Change your mind? Discard edits. When a photo has unsaved edits, a Discard edits entry appears — in the develop pane as a Discard button at the right end of the title row, beside [unsaved] (nothing in the pane moves when it comes and goes), in the ⋯ More menu, and in the DEVELOP / CROP tab palettes. It reverts the whole photo (grade, crop, alignment, snapshots) to the last state you saved to the server, in one click. Alignment points on their own never count as unsaved, so they never bring Discard up — but when you do discard a grade or a crop, the points go back to the last save with the rest. Cmd+Z brings the grade, the crop and the alignment points back, like any edit; a snapshot made since the last save is not part of undo and stays gone. (A photo you never saved reverts to the original.) It's distinct from Reset, which always clears back to zero; Discard goes back to your last save. Inside an open crop, Esc after a discard returns to the discarded-to crop, not to the one you threw away.
  • Undo/redo and snapshots work on Immich photos too, the same as local ones.
  • Just after you move to an Immich photo there's a brief moment (a fraction of a second) while culler reads its saved grade back; if you press J that instant it'll say "still loading" — wait a beat and press it again. Discard waits for the same read, on any photo (guessing before culler knows what the server holds would be wrong), and says the same thing.

Where it all lives

Edits are stored per photo in its sidecar as crs: fields (the full list is in the reference) and never touch the image file. For photos developed on their JPEG, filmstrip thumbnails re-render with your tone baked in, so edited photos are recognizable at a glance; RAW-paired photos keep plain thumbnails (same reason their culling view stays plain) and announce their edits through the [edited] overlay tag instead. The Edited filter (culling) collects both kinds. To turn an edit into an actual JPEG, see export.
